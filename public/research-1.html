The MSG Myth:
how a label outran the facts.

By Joshua Wright  ·  Coming soon

[This is placeholder text.] In 1968, a single letter published in a major medical journal suggested that monosodium glutamate — MSG — might be responsible for a set of symptoms after eating at Chinese restaurants. It wasn't a study. It was one doctor's observation. But it was enough.

Within a few years, "No MSG" had become a marketing badge. Restaurants printed it on menus. Parents avoided it. An entire additive that had been used safely for over half a century became shorthand for something harmful — long before any rigorous science had weighed in.

What the science actually found

Over the following decades, study after study looked for a credible link between MSG and the reported symptoms. Controlled trials — where participants didn't know whether they were getting MSG or a placebo — repeatedly failed to reproduce the effect. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration classifies MSG as "generally recognized as safe."

"The science was always there. But the narrative was stronger." — a pattern that repeats far beyond food.

That's the part that matters for MSG Teens. The MSG story isn't really about food. It's about how fast a label can spread, how long it can stick, and how much harder it is to clear a name than to stain one. Teenagers live inside that same pattern every day.

Why this matters for teens

[Placeholder.] Just like MSG, teenagers carry labels that were assigned quickly and questioned slowly — lazy, disrespectful, distracted, dramatic. The evidence often points the other way, but the story adults already believe is stronger than the facts in front of them. This is the connection every piece of research on this site keeps coming back to.
